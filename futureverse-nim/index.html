🏡 Back to the futureverse: R's futures, explained in Nim

Back to the futureverse

Henrik Bengtsson's futureverse ("you keep your code, and you decide where it runs") lets an R programmer write lapply(xs, slow), add one word, and have it run on all the cores of a laptop, on a few machines, or on a supercomputer, with nothing else changed. This page explains the ideas behind it for someone who knows Nim and only a little R. The best way to understand a design is to build it, so we build a toy futureverse in Nim (285 lines), and every example on this page runs on it when the page is built. Fasten your seatbelt: we travel through a cloakroom, a few time machines and a macro.

The short version
  • A future is a coat-check ticket: you hand in some work, get a ticket at once, and collect the value later. Nim has the same idea in taskpools (spawn/sync) and in std/asyncdispatch (for waiting rather than computing).
  • The big idea is a split of roles: the developer says what may run concurrently, the end-user says how and where with plan(). The code never names a backend.
  • The hard part is making parallel code behave like sequential code: the same random numbers on any number of workers, output and errors arriving in order, progress bars. Our toy does all of it on threads (via malebolgia) and on worker processes.
  • futurize(), the newest piece, rewrites a plain map into a parallel one. In Nim that is just a macro, and we write it: xs.mapIt(it * it) |> futurize(seed = 1).
  • In Nim you need all this less often (compiled loops are fast, and threads share memory), but the split of roles, the seeds and the relaying are worth borrowing.

Just enough R

R is a language for statistics. Two things about it matter here. It is functional: functions are values, and the usual way to loop is to hand a function to a map, lapply(xs, f), which applies f to every element of xs and gives back the list of results. And an R session runs your code on one thread: to use more cores, R starts more R processes and ships the work to them. The little R this page uses, next to its Nim:

Meanwhile, in R

The futureverse is a family of packages: future (the core, 2015), future.apply (parallel lapply and friends), furrr (parallel purrr, by Davis Vaughan), doFuture (for foreach), progressr (progress bars), parallelly (how many cores may I use?), backends such as future.mirai and future.batchtools, and since this year futurize (the subject of the paper Pietro sent) and its sibling progressify. The design is in the R Journal paper of 2021. And it is not niche: according to futureverse.org, as of November 2026 about 1,300 CRAN packages depend on future directly or indirectly (5.1% of CRAN), among them Seurat, mlr3, targets and shiny, and in eleven years it has had no breaking update.

A future is a coat-check ticket

At a Paris cloakroom you hand over your coat, get a numbered ticket at once, and go to the talk. Later you hand back the ticket and get your coat. A future is that ticket, for a computation: creating it is quick, the work happens somewhere else, and asking for its value waits only if the work is not done yet.

Nim has futures too. With taskpools (Status's thread pool) the ticket is a Flowvar and sync collects it. This runs as the page is built, on 4 threads:

proc slowCube(x: int): int =
  sleep 100
  x * x * x

var tp = Taskpool.new(numThreads = 4)
let t0 = getMonoTime()
let a = tp.spawn slowCube(3)       # a ticket, at once
let b = tp.spawn slowCube(4)
echo "two tickets after ", (getMonoTime() - t0).inMilliseconds, " ms"
echo "a + b = ", sync(a) + sync(b), " after ", (getMonoTime() - t0).inMilliseconds, " ms"
tp.shutdown()
two tickets after 0 ms
a + b = 91 after 100 ms

And Nim's standard library has Future[T] in std/asyncdispatch. Same ticket, different cloakroom: these futures run on one thread, and are for waiting (on the network, on a timer) rather than for computing. Three waits of 300, 100 and 200 ms take 300 ms together:

let s0 = getMonoTime()
proc fetch(name: string; ms: int): Future[string] {.async.} =
  await sleepAsync(ms)             # waiting, not working: the thread is free meanwhile
  return name & " ready after " & $(getMonoTime() - s0).inMilliseconds & " ms"

for s in waitFor all(@[fetch("coat", 300), fetch("hat", 100), fetch("scarf", 200)]):
  echo s
coat ready after 300 ms
hat ready after 100 ms
scarf ready after 200 ms
Aside

Henrik's paper makes this distinction in its first footnote: concurrent describes the logical structure ("these can happen in any order"), parallel the physical execution ("these happen at the same time, on different cores"). A future is a concurrency idea; the backend decides whether it becomes parallelism. That is why futures are not only about speed: Shiny apps use them to stay responsive while a slow computation runs in the background, which is exactly the job of Nim's async futures above.

plan(): who stands behind the counter

Here is the idea that makes the futureverse more than a thread pool. A package author writing future() or a parallel map never says where it runs. The person using the code picks that, once, with plan():

The same lapply(...) |> futurize() then runs on a laptop, on a lab's machines, or as Slurm jobs on a cluster. The paper calls this separation of concerns: the developer decides what, the end-user decides how and where. A rule follows from it: packages must never call plan(), because that would take the choice away from the user (the paper found CRAN packages that do it anyway; Henrik has a blog post titled Setting Future Plans in R Functions, and Why You Probably Shouldn't). And every backend must pass the conformance tests of future.tests before it may call itself one, so, in the site's words, "switching backend never changes your results, only where they are computed". Roads? Where this code is going, it doesn't need to know the roads.

Same code, same answer, a quarter of the time. processes(4) starts four copies of this very program, which is the Nim version of R's multisession. It is not much slower than threads here, because starting a compiled program takes a millisecond or two, not the second or so an R session takes. How it works: the program is started again with --fv-worker slowSquare, and fvServe(), the first line of the program, turns it into a worker that reads elements as JSON lines on stdin and writes results on stdout.

Nim

R ships code; Nim ships only data. An R worker is an empty R session, so the future package sends it the expression, the functions it calls and the data they use. A Nim worker is the same compiled binary, so it already has all the code: only the name of the task and the elements travel. That is also why a task must be registered (registerTask slowSquare): the worker looks it up by name.

How many workers? R's parallelly answers with availableCores(), which is not the number of cores of the machine but the number you may use: it reads cgroup quotas (containers), HPC scheduler allocations and a list of environment variables, so a job does not grab all 128 cores of a shared server. fv has a tiny version of it:

Globals: what is in the coat pockets

Look at a task that uses a variable it did not get as an argument:

A worker process does not have rate. So before sending a future, the future package reads the code of the expression (static analysis, with the globals package), finds every name it uses that is not defined inside it, and ships those values along: the coat and what is in its pockets. This is clever and mostly invisible, and it is where most surprises live (an object that cannot be copied to another process, like a database connection, breaks).

In Nim, a thread backend does not ship anything: threads share memory. The danger is the opposite one, two threads touching the same data. Nim's compiler checks it: a proc run on another thread must be gcsafe, so it may not read a global that holds garbage-collected memory. This snippet does not compile, and the compiler names the global:

var rates = @[1.0, 1.2, 1.5]       # a global seq: memory owned by the main thread

proc f(x: int): float {.gcsafe.} =
  x.float * rates[x mod 3]
snippet.nim(3, 6) Error: 'f' is not GC-safe as it accesses 'rates' which is a global using GC'ed memory

So "detect the globals" is a job of the Nim compiler, not of a library, and it is done before the program runs instead of while it runs. The fix is the one R does for you: pass the data as an argument (or make it a const, which is compiled in). The processes plan sits in between: a worker process re-runs the program's top-level code up to fvServe(), so a global computed there exists in the worker too, recomputed, not shipped.

The same check, through futurize (N° 7): a local variable can't sneak in

Random numbers: no time paradoxes

Statisticians parallelize resampling all the time (bootstraps, permutation tests, simulations), so random numbers are central. Two things go wrong when you run them in parallel naively. Results change with the number of workers, because each worker's generator hands out numbers in a different order. And workers seeded carelessly can produce overlapping or correlated streams.

The futureverse fixes both with seed = TRUE: every element gets its own stream (L'Ecuyer's MRG32k3a generator, whose streams are provably far apart), chosen from the element's position. The result then depends only on the seed, never on the plan: change the past (how many workers, which machine) and the future stays the same. fv does the same with a simpler trick: element i seeds its own generator from splitmix64(seed, i).

for p in [sequential(), threads(3), processes(4)]:
  plan p
  let ys = futureMap(toSeq(1..6), noisy, seed = 42)
  echo ($plan()).alignLeft(14), ys.mapIt(it.formatFloat(ffDecimal, 3)).join("  ")
sequential    0.931  0.458  -0.305  -0.989  0.564  0.533
threads(3)    0.931  0.458  -0.305  -0.989  0.564  0.533
processes(4)  0.931  0.458  -0.305  -0.989  0.564  0.533

Identical, to the last bit. And if a task uses random numbers without a seed, you get the futureverse's famous warning, rather than silently unreproducible results:

plan threads(4)

Output and errors come back as if nothing happened

Run a map in parallel with most tools and the side effects get lost or scrambled. In R, parallel::mclapply drops messages and warnings, and returns errors as values; the paper notes that many users assume it behaves like lapply and are not aware. In Nim, echo from threads just interleaves. Here malebolgia spawns eight tasks that print as they finish (the larger x, the shorter the sleep):

proc rawChatty(x: int) =
  sleep (9 - x) * 15
  echo "x = ", x

var m = createMaster()
m.awaitAll:
  for x in 1..8:
    m.spawn rawChatty(x)

x = 6
x = 8
x = 5
x = 4
x = 3
x = 2
x = 1

The futureverse instead captures standard output, messages and warnings on the worker and relays them in the main session, in the order a sequential run would show them, after each future is collected. They are real R conditions again on arrival, so suppressMessages() around the parallel call still works. fv does it with say: each element's lines are kept with its result, and the main thread echoes them in element order:

plan processes(4)
echo futureMap(toSeq(1..4), chatty)
x = 1
x = 2
x = 3
x = 4
@[1.0, 1.4142135623730951, 1.7320508075688772, 2.0]

Errors too: the first failing element (in order) raises its exception on the main thread, where an ordinary try catches it, with its own type:

plan threads(4)
try:
  echo futureMap(toSeq(1..8), fragile)
except ValueError as e:
  echo "caught a ValueError from a worker: ", e.msg
caught a ValueError from a worker: I don't like 3
Careful

fv cannot pass the exception object across: under Nim's ORC memory manager a ref belongs to the thread that made it (a first version did pass it, and crashed with a segfault in the cycle collector). So only the name and message travel, and fv makes a new exception of the same type for the common ones. R serializes the whole condition object, class and all. A Nim library done properly could move the exception with std/isolation.

futurize: where we're going, we don't need future_lapply

Before futurize, parallelizing meant switching function: lapply becomes future.apply::future_lapply, purrr::map becomes furrr::future_map, foreach() %do% becomes %dofuture%, each with its own way of passing options. futurize keeps the code you wrote and adds one word:

It works because R is lazy: futurize() receives its argument unevaluated, as a piece of code (with substitute()), finds which map it is (base::lapply? purrr::map?), and rewrites it into the parallel version before running it. The paper calls this transpiling. It also unwraps { }, local(), suppressMessages() to find the map inside, and knows the parallel switches of domain packages so you don't have to: the site counts 51 of them on CRAN and Bioconductor, from boot and glmnet to DESeq2 and SingleCellExperiment.

Nim does code-as-data at compile time: a macro receives its argument unevaluated, as a syntax tree. So the Nim futurize is a macro that recognizes map(xs, f) and xs.mapIt(expr) from std/sequtils and rewrites them into futureMap. Nim does not have R's |>, but a macro can be an operator, so we add that too, in five lines:

plan threads(4)
let squares = toSeq(1..8).mapIt(it * it + 1) |> futurize(seed = 1)
echo squares
echo toSeq(1..8).map(slowSquare) |> futurize()
@[2, 5, 10, 17, 26, 37, 50, 65]
@[1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64]

Like futurize(eval = FALSE), transpiled shows what the macro writes:

echo transpiled(toSeq(1..8).mapIt(it * it + 1), seed = 1)
block:
  proc fvIt_1040197170(it: typeof(toSeq(1 .. 8)[0])): auto {.nimcall, gcsafe.} =
    it * it + 1

  futureMap(toSeq(1 .. 8), fvIt_1040197170, seed = 1)

Note the {.nimcall, gcsafe.} that the macro adds to the generated proc. It is what gives the compiler error at the end of N° 3: a local variable can't be captured by a proc that runs somewhere else. And R's futurize(FALSE), which turns every futurize off for debugging, is a compile-time switch here: build with -d:futurizeOff and each futurize becomes the plain map.

Meanwhile, in R

One difference of philosophy. In R, only the end-user may switch futurization off, and packages must never do it. In Nim the end-user of a library is whoever compiles the program, so a -d: define is the natural place for that switch, and for the plan too, if you want it fixed at compile time.

So, does Nim need a futureverse?

Partly. Many of the problems the futureverse solves come from R: an interpreter with one thread, where parallel means processes, and processes mean shipping code and data. In Nim a slow loop is often fast enough once compiled, and threads share memory with compiler checks. But some of the ideas have no good Nim equivalent yet:

What a Nim programmer can take from Henrik, in order of how cheap it is:

  1. Seed per element, not per worker. Free, and it makes parallel results reproducible.
  2. Relay, don't print. Keep a task's output and errors with its result, and show them in order. Debugging a parallel program becomes debugging a sequential one.
  3. Don't own the pool. A library should take the executor from its caller (malebolgia's Master passed down is close to this), the way packages leave plan() to the user.
  4. Respect the machine's limits: an availableCores in the standard library.
  5. A test suite for backends. The futureverse has future.tests, which every backend must pass to be called compliant. That is how "any backend works" stays true.
  6. The macro. futurize is a 285-line toy here; a real one would know more maps (collect, for loops with ||), and a Nim cluster backend would be a fun project.

How the toy is built: fv.nim, and its limits
  • Threads: malebolgia's awaitAll/spawn. Each of the n workers gets elements w, w+n, w+2n, ... (R's futures chunk elements too; chunk_size and scheduling tune it). Results go into a preallocated array through a pointer, so no locks.
  • Processes: the running binary, started n times with --fv-worker <task>, elements as JSON lines over pipes (std/json, std/osproc). All elements are sent before any result is read, which is fine for small inputs and would deadlock on big ones.
  • Not done: lazy futures (future(lazy = TRUE)), resolved(), cancellation, timeouts, remote machines, nested plans (R's plan(list(cluster, multicore))), warnings as a kind of their own, and moving exception objects. Toy, remember.
  • Where R is going next (the roadmap): restarting futures whose worker died, resource declarations (memory, run time, GPUs) so a future goes only where it fits, availableMemory() and availableTime() in parallelly, shared memory between local workers. Each would make a nice Nim experiment.

## fv: a toy "futureverse" for Nim, to explain Henrik Bengtsson's R futureverse by building it.
##
## - `plan(sequential())`, `plan(threads(4))`, `plan(processes(4))`: the end-user picks HOW and WHERE.
## - `futureMap(xs, f)`: the developer says WHAT can run concurrently (R: future.apply / furrr).
## - `xs.map(f).futurize(seed = 42)`: a macro that rewrites a sequential map (R: futurize).
## - Inside a task: `say` (output, relayed in order), `gauss`/`dice` (random numbers, reproducible
##   per element with `seed`), exceptions (re-raised on the main thread), `progress = true`.
##
## Threads come from malebolgia (structured: every spawn is awaited inside `awaitAll`).
## Processes are copies of the running program, started with `--fv-worker <task>`: a program that
## uses `processes` must call `registerTask f` for its tasks and then `fvServe()`, before anything else.

# ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- how many cores?
proc availableCores*(): int =
  ## Like parallelly::availableCores(): the cores this program may use, not the cores the
  ## machine has. Honours `FV_AVAILABLECORES` and a Linux cgroup CPU quota (containers, CI).
  result = countProcessors()
  if existsEnv("FV_AVAILABLECORES"):
    return max(1, getEnv("FV_AVAILABLECORES").parseInt)
  try:
    let q = readFile("/sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.max").splitWhitespace
    if q.len == 2 and q[0] != "max":
      result = min(result, max(1, (q[0].parseInt + q[1].parseInt - 1) div q[1].parseInt))
  except IOError, ValueError: discard
  result = max(1, result)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- the plan
proc sequential*(): Plan = Plan(kind: bkSequential, workers: 1)
proc threads*(workers = 0): Plan =
  ## Threads of this process (malebolgia's pool, at most `ThreadPoolSize` of them).
  Plan(kind: bkThreads, workers: min(if workers > 0: workers else: availableCores(), ThreadPoolSize))
proc processes*(workers = 0): Plan =
  ## Copies of this program, talking JSON over pipes (R's `multisession`, without R).
  Plan(kind: bkProcesses, workers: if workers > 0: workers else: availableCores())

proc plan*(p: Plan) = currentPlan = p
proc plan*(): Plan = currentPlan
proc `$`*(p: Plan): string =
  if p.kind == bkSequential: "sequential" else: $p.kind & "(" & $p.workers & ")"

template withPlan*(p: Plan; body: untyped) =
  ## Use a plan for a block only, then put the previous one back (R: `with(plan(...), local = TRUE)`).
  let saved = plan()
  plan(p)
  try: body
  finally: plan(saved)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- inside a task
proc say*(args: varargs[string, `$`]) =
  ## Output from a task. It is captured and relayed by the main thread, in element order, as if
  ## the map ran sequentially (R relays cat(), message() and warning() the same way).
  let s = args.join("")
  if ctx.active: ctx.log.add s else: echo s

proc gauss*(): float =
  ## A standard normal random number from the element's own stream.
  ctx.rngUsed = true
  ctx.rng.gauss()

proc dice*(): int =
  ## A roll of a six-sided die, from the element's own stream.
  ctx.rngUsed = true
  ctx.rng.rand(1..6)

proc mix(seed, i: int): int64 =
  ## splitmix64 of (seed, i): one independent-looking stream per element, whatever runs it.
  var z = cast[uint64](seed) + 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15'u64 * uint64(i + 1)
  z = (z xor (z shr 30)) * 0xBF58476D1CE4E5B9'u64
  z = (z xor (z shr 27)) * 0x94D049BB133111EB'u64
  cast[int64](z xor (z shr 31))

proc runOne[T, U](f: proc(x: T): U {.nimcall, gcsafe.}; x: T; i, seed: int; o: var Outcome[U]) =
  ## Evaluate one element: set up its context, call f, keep what it left behind.
  ctx = Ctx(active: true)
  ctx.rng = if seed >= 0: initRand(mix(seed, i)) else: initRand()   # no seed: from the clock
  try:
    o.value = f(x)
  except CatchableError:
    let e = getCurrentException()
    (o.errName, o.errMsg) = ($e.name, e.msg)
  o.log = move ctx.log
  o.rngUsed = ctx.rngUsed
  ctx.active = false

proc tick() {.gcsafe.} =
  ## One more element done: draw the progress bar when a new tenth is reached.
  let done = progressDone.fetchAdd(1) + 1
  {.cast(gcsafe).}:
    let total = progressTotal
    if total > 0 and done * 10 div total != (done - 1) * 10 div total:
      withLock progressLock:
        let tenths = done * 10 div total
        echo "progress |", "█".repeat(tenths), "·".repeat(10 - tenths), "| ", done, "/", total

proc registerImpl[T, U](name: string; f: proc(x: T): U {.nimcall, gcsafe.}) =
  names[cast[pointer](f)] = name
  registry[name] = Entry(name: name, run: proc(x: JsonNode; i, seed: int): JsonNode =
    var o: Outcome[U]
    runOne(f, x.to(T), i, seed, o)
    result = %*{"i": i, "log": o.log, "rng": o.rngUsed}
    if o.errName.len > 0: result["err"] = %*{"name": o.errName, "msg": o.errMsg}
    else: result["v"] = %o.value)

template registerTask*(f: typed) =
  ## Make `f` callable by worker processes (they look it up by name).
  registerImpl(astToStr(f), f)

proc fvServe*() =
  ## If this program was started as a worker (`--fv-worker <task>`), serve: read one JSON element
  ## per line on stdin, answer one JSON line on stdout, then quit. Otherwise do nothing.
  if paramCount() >= 2 and paramStr(1) == "--fv-worker":
    let e = registry[paramStr(2)]
    for line in stdin.lines:
      let j = parseJson(line)
      stdout.writeLine $e.run(j["x"], j["i"].getInt, j["seed"].getInt)
      stdout.flushFile
    quit 0

proc mapProcesses[T, U](xs: openArray[T]; f: proc(x: T): U {.nimcall, gcsafe.}; seed, workers: int;
                        progress: bool; outs: var seq[Outcome[U]]) =
  let key = cast[pointer](f)
  if key notin names:
    raise newException(ValueError, "processes need `registerTask f` and `fvServe()` at the top of the program")
  let n = min(workers, xs.len)
  var ps: seq[Process]
  for w in 0 ..< n:
    ps.add startProcess(getAppFilename(), args = ["--fv-worker", names[key]], options = {})
  for i, x in xs:     # element i goes to worker i mod n; all input is sent before any is read
    ps[i mod n].inputStream.writeLine $(%*{"i": i, "x": %x, "seed": seed})
  for p in ps: p.inputStream.close
  for p in ps:
    for line in p.outputStream.lines:
      let j = parseJson(line)
      let i = j["i"].getInt
      outs[i].log = j["log"].to(seq[string])
      outs[i].rngUsed = j["rng"].getBool
      if j.hasKey("err"):
        (outs[i].errName, outs[i].errMsg) = (j["err"]["name"].getStr, j["err"]["msg"].getStr)
      else:
        outs[i].value = j["v"].to(U)
      if progress: tick()
    discard p.waitForExit
    p.close

proc relayError(name, msg: string) =
  ## Raise again, on the main thread, an exception a task raised. The object itself can't cross
  ## (under ORC a ref belongs to the thread that made it), so fv makes a new one of the same
  ## type for the common types of the standard library, and a RelayedError for the others.
  template re(T: typedesc) = raise newException(T, msg)
  case name
  of "ValueError": re ValueError
  of "KeyError": re KeyError
  of "IOError": re IOError
  of "OSError": re OSError
  of "ArithmeticError", "DivByZeroDefect": re ArithmeticError
  else:
    let e = newException(RelayedError, "[" & name & "] " & msg)
    e.origName = name
    raise e

proc runChunk[T, U](f: proc(x: T): U {.nimcall, gcsafe.}; xs: ptr UncheckedArray[T];
                    outs: ptr UncheckedArray[Outcome[U]]; lo, hi, step, seed: int; progress: bool) {.gcsafe.} =
  var i = lo
  while i < hi:
    runOne(f, xs[i], i, seed, outs[i])
    if progress: tick()
    i += step

proc futureMap*[T, U](xs: openArray[T]; f: proc(x: T): U {.nimcall, gcsafe.};
                      seed = -1; progress = false): seq[U] =
  ## `xs.map(f)`, evaluated by the current plan. Output from `say` is relayed in element order;
  ## the first exception (in element order) is raised again here; with `seed` set, the random
  ## numbers of element i depend only on (seed, i), so every plan gives the same result.
  var outs = newSeq[Outcome[U]](xs.len)
  progressDone.store(0)
  progressTotal = if progress: xs.len else: 0
  let p = plan()
  if xs.len > 0:
    case p.kind
    of bkSequential:
      runChunk(f, cast[ptr UncheckedArray[T]](unsafeAddr xs[0]), cast[ptr UncheckedArray[Outcome[U]]](addr outs[0]),
               0, xs.len, 1, seed, progress)
    of bkThreads:
      var m = createMaster()
      let px = cast[ptr UncheckedArray[T]](unsafeAddr xs[0])
      let po = cast[ptr UncheckedArray[Outcome[U]]](addr outs[0])
      m.awaitAll:
        for w in 0 ..< min(p.workers, xs.len):     # worker w takes elements w, w+n, w+2n, ...
          m.spawn runChunk(f, px, po, w, xs.len, min(p.workers, xs.len), seed, progress)
    of bkProcesses:
      mapProcesses(xs, f, seed, p.workers, progress, outs)
  progressTotal = 0
  result = newSeq[U](xs.len)
  var warned = false
  for i in 0 ..< outs.len:          # relay, in order, as if it had run sequentially
    for line in outs[i].log: echo line
    if outs[i].errName.len > 0: relayError(outs[i].errName, outs[i].errMsg)
    if outs[i].rngUsed and seed < 0 and not warned:
      echo "Warning: UNRELIABLE VALUE: element ", i + 1, " used random numbers without a seed. ",
           "Pass a seed to get the same numbers on every plan."
      warned = true
    result[i] = move outs[i].value

proc pipe(lhs, rhs: NimNode): NimNode =
  ## `lhs |> rhs`: insert lhs as the first argument of the call rhs.
  if rhs.kind in nnkCallKinds:
    result = rhs.copyNimTree
    result.insert(1, lhs)
  else:
    result = newCall(rhs, lhs)

proc rewrite(call: NimNode; opts: seq[NimNode]): NimNode =
  ## The transpiler: `map(xs, f)` and `xs.mapIt(expr)`, from std/sequtils, become `futureMap`.
  ## It unwraps `( )` and a `progressify(...)` (which becomes `progress = true`).
  var n = if call.kind in {nnkPar, nnkStmtListExpr} and call.len == 1: call[0] else: call
  var opts = opts
  if n.kind == nnkInfix and n[0].eqIdent("|>"):   # an inner pipe reaches the macro unexpanded
    n = pipe(n[1], n[2])
  if n.kind in nnkCallKinds and n.len == 2 and n[0].eqIdent("progressify"):
    n = n[1]
    opts.add nnkExprEqExpr.newTree(ident"progress", newLit(true))
  var fn, xs, arg: NimNode
  if n.kind in nnkCallKinds and n.len == 2 and n[0].kind == nnkDotExpr:   # xs.map(f)
    (fn, xs, arg) = (n[0][1], n[0][0], n[1])
  elif n.kind in nnkCallKinds and n.len == 3:                            # map(xs, f)
    (fn, xs, arg) = (n[0], n[1], n[2])
  else:
    error("futurize: expected map(xs, f) or xs.mapIt(expr), got " & n.repr, n)
  if fn.eqIdent("map"):
    result = newCall(bindSym"futureMap", xs, arg)
  elif fn.eqIdent("mapIt"):
    let it = ident"it"
    let anon = genSym(nskProc, "fvIt")
    result = quote do:
      block:
        proc `anon`(`it`: typeof(`xs`[0])): auto {.nimcall, gcsafe.} = `arg`
        futureMap(`xs`, `anon`)
    let callNode = result[1][1]
    for o in opts: callNode.add o
    return
  else:
    error("futurize: no transpiler for " & fn.repr & " (known: map, mapIt)", n)
  for o in opts: result.add o

macro futurize*(call: untyped; opts: varargs[untyped]): untyped =
  ## `xs.map(f).futurize()`, `xs.mapIt(it * it).futurize(seed = 1)`: run a sequential map with
  ## the current plan. Compile with `-d:futurizeOff` and every futurize is the plain map again.
  when defined(futurizeOff):
    result = call
  else:
    result = rewrite(call, toSeq(opts))

macro progressify*(call: untyped): untyped =
  ## `xs.map(f) |> progressify()`: the same map, with a progress bar, run sequentially (R's
  ## progressify package). Before a futurize, `|> progressify() |> futurize()`, it is unwrapped
  ## by futurize instead, and the bar comes from the workers.
  let m = rewrite(call, @[nnkExprEqExpr.newTree(ident"progress", newLit(true))])
  result = quote do:
    block:
      let saved = plan()
      plan(sequential())
      try: `m`
      finally: plan(saved)

macro transpiled*(call: untyped; opts: varargs[untyped]): string =
  ## What futurize turns `call` into, as source (R: `futurize(eval = FALSE)`).
  newLit(rewrite(call, toSeq(opts)).repr)

macro `|>`*(lhs, rhs: untyped): untyped =
  ## R's native pipe: `x |> f(y)` is `f(x, y)`. So `xs.mapIt(it * 2) |> futurize(seed = 1)` reads
  ## like the R original, and the map reaches the macro unevaluated, as R's `substitute()` sees it.
  pipe(lhs, rhs)

that is not defined inside it, and ships those values along: the coat *and* what is in its
pockets. This is clever and mostly invisible, and it is where most surprises live (an object
that cannot be copied to another process, like a database connection, breaks).

In Nim, a thread backend does not ship anything: threads share memory. The danger is the
opposite one, two threads touching the same data. Nim's compiler checks it: a proc run on
another thread must be `gcsafe`, so it may not read a global that holds garbage-collected
memory. This snippet does not compile, and the compiler names the global:
"""

nbCompileError("""
var rates = @[1.0, 1.2, 1.5]       # a global seq: memory owned by the main thread

proc f(x: int): float {.gcsafe.} =
  x.float * rates[x mod 3]
""", targs = checkArgs)

nbText: """
So "detect the globals" is a job of the Nim compiler, not of a library, and it is done before
the program runs instead of while it runs. The fix is the one R does for you: pass the data as
an argument (or make it a `const`, which is compiled in). The processes plan sits in between:
a worker process re-runs the program's top-level code up to `fvServe()`, so a global computed
there exists in the worker too, recomputed, not shipped.
"""

nbCompileError("""
import std/sequtils, fv
proc main() =
  let rate = 3
  echo toSeq(1..4).mapIt(it * rate) |> futurize()
main()
""", targs = checkArgs, tcmd = "c --compileOnly", tfold = "The same check, through `futurize` (N° 7): a local variable can't sneak in")

# ============================================================================================ 4
nbText: """
## Random numbers: no time paradoxes

Statisticians parallelize resampling all the time (bootstraps, permutation tests,
simulations), so random numbers are central. Two things go wrong when you run them in parallel
naively. Results change with the number of workers, because each worker's generator hands out
numbers in a different order. And workers seeded carelessly can produce overlapping or
correlated streams.

The futureverse fixes both with `seed = TRUE`: every *element* gets its own stream (L'Ecuyer's
MRG32k3a generator, whose streams are provably far apart), chosen from the element's position.
The result then depends only on the seed, never on the plan: change the past (how many workers,
which machine) and the future stays the same. `fv` does the same with a simpler
trick: element `i` seeds its own generator from `splitmix64(seed, i)`.
"""

nbCode:
  for p in [sequential(), threads(3), processes(4)]:
    plan p
    let ys = futureMap(toSeq(1..6), noisy, seed = 42)
    echo ($plan()).alignLeft(14), ys.mapIt(it.formatFloat(ffDecimal, 3)).join("  ")

block:   # the claim below, checked at build time
  var draws: seq[seq[float]]
  for p in [sequential(), threads(3), processes(4)]:
    withPlan p: draws.add futureMap(toSeq(1..6), noisy, seed = 42)
  doAssert draws[0] == draws[1] and draws[1] == draws[2]
nbText: "Identical, to the last bit. And if a task uses random numbers *without* a seed, you get the futureverse's famous warning, rather than silently unreproducible results:"

nbCode:
  plan threads(4)
  discard futureMap(toSeq(1..6), noisy)

nbNote(nkR, """
R prints `UNRELIABLE VALUE: Future (‘<none>’) unexpectedly generated random numbers without
specifying argument 'seed'.` For `replicate()`, which is nearly always used for resampling,
futurize turns `seed = TRUE` on by itself.
""")

# ============================================================================================ 5
nbText: """
## Output and errors come back as if nothing happened

Run a map in parallel with most tools and the side effects get lost or scrambled. In R,
`parallel::mclapply` drops messages and warnings, and returns errors as values; the paper notes
that many users assume it behaves like `lapply` and are not aware. In Nim, `echo` from threads
just interleaves. Here malebolgia spawns eight tasks that print as they finish (the larger x,
the shorter the sleep):
"""

nbCode:
  proc rawChatty(x: int) =
    sleep (9 - x) * 15
    echo "x = ", x

  var m = createMaster()
  m.awaitAll:
    for x in 1..8:
      m.spawn rawChatty(x)

nbText: """
The futureverse instead **captures** standard output, messages and warnings on the worker and
**relays** them in the main session, in the order a sequential run would show them, after each
future is collected. They are real R conditions again on arrival, so `suppressMessages()` around
the parallel call still works. `fv` does it with `say`: each element's lines are kept with its
result, and the main thread echoes them in element order:
"""

nbCode:
  plan processes(4)
  echo futureMap(toSeq(1..4), chatty)

nbText: """
Errors too: the first failing element (in order) raises its exception on the main thread, where
an ordinary `try` catches it, with its own type:
"""

nbCode:
  plan threads(4)
  try:
    echo futureMap(toSeq(1..8), fragile)
  except ValueError as e:
    echo "caught a ValueError from a worker: ", e.msg

nbNote(nkCareful, """
`fv` cannot pass the exception *object* across: under Nim's ORC memory manager a ref belongs to
the thread that made it (a first version did pass it, and crashed with a segfault in the cycle
collector). So only the name and message travel, and `fv` makes a new exception of the same type
for the common ones. R serializes the whole condition object, class and all. A Nim library done
properly could move the exception with `std/isolation`.
""")

nbText: """
## Progress from far away

Long maps want a progress bar, and the bar must be in the main session while the work is in the
workers. Henrik's **progressr** solves it the R way: `p()` in the task *signals a condition*,
and the future backends relay those near-live, as they happen, not at the end. `fv` takes a
shortcut, `progress = true` ticks once per element from whichever thread finished it:
"""

nbCode:
  plan threads(4)
  discard futureMap(toSeq(1..20), slowSquare, progress = true)

nbText: """
The paper listed a `progressify()` as future work; by now it has shipped. It injects the `p()`
calls itself, so `lapply(xs, slow) |> progressify() |> futurize()` gets a bar with no other
change. `fv` has one too, a macro that the futurize macro unwraps (one of each is enough):
"""

nbCode:
  plan threads(4)
  discard toSeq(1..10).map(slowSquare) |> progressify() |> futurize()

nbText: "Which brings us to the macros."

# ============================================================================================ 7
nbText: &"""
## futurize: where we're going, we don't need future_lapply

Before futurize, parallelizing meant switching function: `lapply` becomes
`future.apply::future_lapply`, `purrr::map` becomes `furrr::future_map`, `foreach() %do%`
becomes `%dofuture%`, each with its own way of passing options. [futurize]({futurizeRepo})
keeps the code you wrote and adds one word:
"""

nbR("""
ys <- lapply(xs, slow_fcn) |> futurize()
ys <- map(xs, rnorm, n = 10) |> futurize(seed = TRUE)
b  <- boot(bigcity, ratio, R = 999, stype = "w") |> futurize()
""")

nbText: """
It works because R is lazy: `futurize()` receives its argument *unevaluated*, as a piece of
code (with `substitute()`), finds which map it is (`base::lapply`? `purrr::map`?), and rewrites
it into the parallel version before running it. The paper calls this **transpiling**. It also
unwraps `{ }`, `local()`, `suppressMessages()` to find the map inside, and knows the parallel
switches of domain packages so you don't have to: the site counts 51 of them on CRAN and
Bioconductor, from boot and glmnet to DESeq2 and SingleCellExperiment.

Nim does code-as-data at compile time: a **macro** receives its argument unevaluated, as a syntax
tree. So the Nim futurize is a macro that recognizes `map(xs, f)` and `xs.mapIt(expr)` from
`std/sequtils` and rewrites them into `futureMap`. Nim does not have R's `|>`, but a macro can be
an operator, so we add that too, in five lines:
"""

nbCode:
  plan threads(4)
  let squares = toSeq(1..8).mapIt(it * it + 1) |> futurize(seed = 1)
  echo squares
  echo toSeq(1..8).map(slowSquare) |> futurize()

nbText: "Like `futurize(eval = FALSE)`, `transpiled` shows what the macro writes:"

nbCode:
  echo transpiled(toSeq(1..8).mapIt(it * it + 1), seed = 1)

nbText: """
Note the `{.nimcall, gcsafe.}` that the macro adds to the generated proc. It is what gives the
compiler error at the end of N° 3: a local variable can't be captured by a proc that runs
somewhere else. And R's `futurize(FALSE)`, which turns every futurize off for debugging, is a
compile-time switch here: build with `-d:futurizeOff` and each futurize becomes the plain map.
"""

nbNote(nkR, """
One difference of philosophy. In R, only the **end-user** may switch futurization off, and
packages must never do it. In Nim the end-user of a library is whoever compiles the program,
so a `-d:` define is the natural place for that switch, and for the plan too, if you want it
fixed at compile time.
""")

What a Nim programmer can take from Henrik, in order of how cheap it is:

1. **Seed per element, not per worker.** Free, and it makes parallel results reproducible.
2. **Relay, don't print.** Keep a task's output and errors with its result, and show them in
   order. Debugging a parallel program becomes debugging a sequential one.
3. **Don't own the pool.** A library should take the executor from its caller (malebolgia's
   `Master` passed down is close to this), the way packages leave `plan()` to the user.
4. **Respect the machine's limits**: an `availableCores` in the standard library.
5. **A test suite for backends.** The futureverse has `future.tests`, which every backend must
   pass to be called compliant. That is how "any backend works" stays true.
6. **The macro.** `futurize` is a {fvLines}-line toy here; a real one would know more maps
   (`collect`, `for` loops with `||`), and a Nim cluster backend would be a fun project.
