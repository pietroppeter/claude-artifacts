Moiré: when two patterns make a third

Lay one striped sheet over another and a third pattern appears that neither sheet has: broad, slow, ghostly bands. That is a moiré. It is a nuisance when you photograph a screen, and a superpower when you want to see a movement far too small to see: slide one sheet by a hair and the bands sweep by a hand's width.

Everything below is for your finger. Drag, slide, tap.

The word is French: moiré is watered silk, a fabric pressed in two folded layers so that their threads beat against each other and leave a shimmering, wood-grain look. The physics is the same in every case below: two regular patterns, almost but not quite alike, and an eye (or a camera, or a sensor) that cannot see the fine detail and sees only the slow difference.

Two rhythms, one beat

Start with the simplest case: two sets of parallel lines, the same orientation, slightly different spacing. Where the lines coincide, light gets through the gaps of both sheets; half a beat later each sheet's lines fill the other's gaps, and the overlap goes dark.

It is the visual twin of two guitar strings slightly out of tune: two notes of 440 and 442 Hz throb twice a second. Here, frequency is lines per unit of length.

P = p1 p2 / |p1 − p2|the fringe period: 1/P is the difference of the two line frequencies 1/p₁ − 1/p₂

That formula is in lib/moiremath.nim, the one Nim module that both this page (when it was built) and the widgets (in your browser) use:

The idea

Multiply two waves and you get their sum frequency and their difference frequency. The sum is even finer than the lines, so your eye blurs it away; the difference is coarse, and that is what you see. Moiré is the difference frequency, made visible.

Turn one sheet

Now take two identical sheets and turn one of them. At zero degrees nothing happens; a small turn makes huge fringes, running almost at right angles to the lines; the more you turn, the finer they get.

P = p / (2 sin(θ/2)) ≈ p / θsmall angles: one degree turns a grating into fringes about 57 times wider

The same formula, at the scale of atoms, is one of the big stories of recent physics. Graphene is a single sheet of carbon atoms in a honeycomb with a spacing of 0.246 nm. Stack two sheets and twist one by about 1.1°, the "magic angle", and the moiré of the two lattices is a new, much larger lattice about 12.8 nm across. Electrons feel it, and in 2018 the twisted pair turned out to be a superconductor (twisted bilayer graphene).

The lever: see a movement twenty times bigger

Here is the trick that makes moiré useful. Take two sheets with periods 8 and 8.4, and slide the top one by a small amount d. The fringes do not move by d: they move by d · p₁/(p₁ − p₂), here 20. times as far, and the other way.

That is a lever with no moving parts, and no friction. You have seen it before on a caliper: a vernier scale puts 10 marks over the length of 9 main-scale marks, and the mark that lines up tells you the tenth of a millimetre. A vernier is a one-dimensional moiré, read by eye.

Rings, rays and lines

Moiré is not only about straight lines. Any two regular patterns will do, and the fringes draw the curves along which the two patterns are "in step". Two sets of circles make hyperbolas and ellipses; circles over lines make parabolas; two sets of rays make circles.

Aliasing: the moiré inside your camera

A screen, a camera sensor and this very page are grids too: rows of pixels. Draw a pattern finer than the pixels and the pixel grid beats against it exactly like a second sheet. Signal processing calls it aliasing: a fine pattern, sampled too coarsely, pretends to be a coarse one.

The picture below is a zone plate: rings that get finer and finer away from the centre. Make the pixels bigger and watch rings appear that are not in the picture. Then switch to averaging, which is what an anti-aliasing filter (or a camera's slightly blurry optical low-pass filter) does: the ghosts fade to grey instead.

Where do the ghosts start? Where a ring is less than two pixels wide (the Nyquist limit):

A glacier in stripes

In the summer of 1842, James David Forbes planted a line of marks straight across the Mer de Glace, the great glacier under Mont Blanc, and came back to survey them again and again. The line did not stay straight. It bowed downstream: the ice in the middle of the glacier flows faster than the ice by the rock walls, which drags along them.

Moiré turns that one survey line into a whole map. Paint a grating of lines across the ice (the black sheet, "before"), let the glacier carry it (the blue sheet, "after"), and lay the two over each other. Wherever the ice has moved by exactly one line spacing, the two sheets line up again: each fringe is a contour of equal movement.

The flow across the valley here is a toy, but a reasonable one: speed u(y) = umax (1 − |y/W|4), the shape that Glen's flow law (ice as a fluid that softens under stress, exponent n ≈ 3) gives a glacier that hardly slides on its bed. The widget uses a gentler parabola instead, 1 − |y/W|², the shape for a runny, honey-like fluid (n = 1): real ice is flatter in the middle and shears in a narrow band by the walls, which would squeeze all the fringes against the rock.

Real glaciers are mapped the same way, with more sophisticated sheets. Satellites compare two images of the ice taken days apart: optical images by matching patches of texture (feature tracking, image correlation), radar images by their phase. In radar interferometry the two radar waves themselves are the gratings, and the interferogram is a moiré made of rainbow fringes: with the C-band radar of the Sentinel-1 satellites (wavelength 5.55 cm), each fringe is 2.77 cm of movement toward or away from the satellite.

## The few formulas of moiré, in plain Nim. The page runs them when it is built (the numbers
## in the text and tables) and the widgets run the very same procs in the browser (`nim js`).
import std/math

func moirePeriod*(p1, p2: float): float =
  ## Two parallel gratings of periods p1 and p2 beat with period p1·p2/|p1−p2|
  ## (the difference of their spatial frequencies 1/p1 − 1/p2). Equal periods: no fringes (inf).
  if p1 == p2: Inf else: p1 * p2 / abs(p1 - p2)

func rotationPeriod*(p, theta: float): float =
  ## Two identical gratings of period p, one turned by theta radians: fringes p / (2·sin(θ/2)),
  ## roughly p/θ for small angles.
  if theta == 0: Inf else: p / (2 * sin(abs(theta) / 2))

func magnification*(p1, p2: float): float =
  ## Slide the grating of period p2 by d: the fringes move by d·p1/(p1 − p2) (signed).
  ## A lever with no moving parts: the vernier of a caliper is the same idea.
  if p1 == p2: Inf else: p1 / (p1 - p2)

func grating*(u, p: float; duty = 0.5; soft = 0.0): float =
  ## Transmission (1 clear, 0 ink) at position u of a line grating of period p. `duty` is the
  ## clear fraction; `soft` (same units as u) blurs the edges so the screen itself does not alias.
  let x = u - p * floor(u / p)             # where we are inside one period, 0 ≤ x < p
  let h = duty * p / 2                      # half the clear part, centred at h
  var d = abs(x - h)
  d = min(d, p - d)                         # distance from the centre of the nearest clear part
  if soft <= 0: (if d < h: 1.0 else: 0.0)
  else: clamp((h - d) / soft + 0.5, 0.0, 1.0)

func flow*(y, halfWidth, uMax: float; n = 3.0): float =
  ## A toy glacier: surface speed across the valley, fastest in the middle (y = 0), still at the
  ## walls (|y| = halfWidth). n ≈ 3 is the shape Glen's flow law gives a glacier sliding little.
  let s = clamp(abs(y) / halfWidth, 0.0, 1.0)
  uMax * (1 - pow(s, n + 1))

func insarFringe*(wavelength: float): float =
  ## Radar interferometry: one fringe (a full colour cycle) is half the radar wavelength of
  ## movement toward or away from the satellite (the signal goes there and back).
  wavelength / 2

proc one(c: Rgb; t: float): int {.inline.} =
  ## One sheet: ink c where t = 0, paper where t = 1.

proc draw(w: Widget) =
  let cw = w.canvas.cssWidth
  if cw <= 0: return
  # shades work in a frame 360 units wide, whatever the canvas's size: s = device px per unit
  let s = dpr() * cw / 360
  let W = int(360 * s)
  let H = int(360 * w.aspect * s)
  w.canvas.setW W
  w.canvas.setH H
  let ctx = w.canvas.getContext2d
  let img = ctx.createImageData(W, H)
  let px = img.data
  if w.blk <= 1:
    for j in 0 ..< H:
      let y = (float(j) + 0.5) / s
      for i in 0 ..< W:
        let c = w.shade((float(i) + 0.5) / s, y)
        let k = 4 * (j * W + i)
        px[k] = (c shr 16) and 255; px[k + 1] = (c shr 8) and 255; px[k + 2] = c and 255; px[k + 3] = 255
  else:
    let b = max(1, int(w.blk * s))      # block size in device pixels
    var j0 = 0
    while j0 < H:
      var i0 = 0
      while i0 < W:
        var c: Rgb
        proc unpack(v: int): Rgb = [float((v shr 16) and 255), float((v shr 8) and 255), float(v and 255)]
        if w.smooth:
          const n = 4
          for a in 0 ..< n:
            for bb in 0 ..< n:
              let d = unpack w.shade((float(i0) + (float(a) + 0.5) * float(b) / n) / s,
                              (float(j0) + (float(bb) + 0.5) * float(b) / n) / s)
              for q in 0..2: c[q] += d[q] / (n * n)
        else:
          c = unpack w.shade((float(i0) + float(b) / 2) / s, (float(j0) + float(b) / 2) / s)
        for j in j0 ..< min(j0 + b, H):

            px[k] = int(c[0]); px[k + 1] = int(c[1]); px[k + 2] = int(c[2]); px[k + 3] = 255
        i0 += b
      j0 += b

proc beats() =
  var p1 = 8.0
  var p2 = 9.0
  # four bands: A, B, A×B, and A×B seen from afar (blurred over one period)
  let w = widget("beat", 0.72, proc (x, y: float): int =
    let a = grating(x, p1, soft = soft, duty = duty)
    let b = grating(x, p2, soft = soft, duty = duty)
    if y < 40: one(ink, a)
    elif y < 48: paperC
    elif y < 88: one(ink2, b)
    elif y < 100: paperC
    elif y < 180: two(ink, a, ink2, b)
    elif y < 196: paperC
    else:
      # what the eye sees from a distance: the average of A×B over one period around x
      const n = 48
      var acc = 0.0
      let span = max(p1, p2)
      for k in 0 ..< n:
        let xx = x + (float(k) / n - 0.5) * span
        acc += grating(xx, p1, duty) * grating(xx, p2, duty)
      let t = acc / n
      # as a curve: dark under the line, paper above
      let top = 196.0
      let hgt = 64.0
      let yv = top + hgt * (1 - (t - (2 * duty - 1)) / (1 - duty))   # the average goes from 2·duty−1 to duty
      if y > yv: one(mark, 0.35) else: paperC)
  proc update() =
    let P = moirePeriod(p1, p2)
    say("beat-out", &"fringe period <b>{fmt1(P)} units</b> = {fmt1(p1)}·{fmt1(p2)} / |{fmt1(p1)} − {fmt1(p2)}|")
