Diagrams, part two

Diagrams nobody has to think about

Tufte taught us to craft one graphic with great care. Mermaid and Pele do something else: they make the box-and-arrow drawing everybody already knows how to read, so the author thinks only about the content. That other tradition has its own pioneer, and this page is drawn in his style.

1Two traditions

Every diagram sits somewhere between two poles. At one end is the graphic made for this data and this argument only. Its maker chooses every mark, and the reader is expected to study it. At the other end is the diagram whose form is already agreed: a flowchart, a sequence diagram, an org chart, a metro map. Nobody admires its design, because the design has disappeared into a convention. You read the content directly.

The crafted graphic

Tufte’s line

Minard’s march, Playfair’s trade balance, a sparkline in a sentence. Each is a one-off solution. The goal is maximum data per drop of ink for a reader who will look closely. The tools that serve it are toolkits for building marks: D3, Vega-Lite, ggplot2, Observable Plot. They give you full control and expect you to use it.

The standard diagram

Neurath’s line

Isotype charts, Beck’s Underground, the ISO flowchart symbols, UML, C4. The value is in the shared form: once you know the convention, you read every example of it without effort. The tools that serve it are notations with a renderer: Graphviz, PlantUML, Mermaid, D2, Pele. They take control away from you, on purpose.

The word “industrial” fits better than it sounds. Neurath ran picture-making as a workshop with a division of labour: researchers collected the data, a transformer turned it into a chart design following fixed rules, and artists drew it with a library of standard symbols. Mermaid and Pele automate the transformer. Researchers call these forms visual notations. Practitioners say diagrams as code. “Everyday diagrams” would also work.

WHAT: the labels and content. LINKS: which things connect. KIND: which notation (flowchart, sequence, ER…). PLACE: positions and sizes. ROUTE: the path of each arrow. LOOK: colours, fonts, shapes. The placement is a judgment: draw.io routes connectors automatically, Graphviz lets you style every node, and in C4 the model fixes the diagram kinds.

Going down the chart, you hand over more decisions, and the diagram gets cheaper to make and to change. Mermaid’s bet is that for most diagrams in a README, a design doc or a chat answer, the last three decisions are not worth a human’s time.

You place the boxes
An algorithm places them

Mermaid and draw.io are not really competitors: they sit in opposite corners. draw.io is for when the position of every box matters, and its files are XML that nobody reads in a diff. Mermaid is for when the relations matter and positions don’t. Mermaid’s real competitors are in its own corner: PlantUML for UML, D2 for architecture, Structurizr for C4, Graphviz for anything graph-shaped. Pele competes with Mermaid’s renderer, not its language.

4What Mermaid and Pele do differently

  1. The first word picks a convention. flowchart, sequenceDiagram, erDiagram, gantt, gitGraph, mindmap: more than twenty diagram kinds, each with its layout rules built in. You choose a notation, not a drawing.
  2. You write relations, never positions. A --> B says that A leads to B. Where A ends up on the page is not something you can say, so it is not something you have to think about.
  3. An algorithm lays it out. Flowcharts use layered (Sugiyama) layout, sequence diagrams lay themselves out on a timeline, Gantt charts on a calendar. Pele brings “its own layout and visual style” and adapts to the container’s width.
  4. The source sits with the content. It goes in a Markdown fence, gets reviewed in a diff, and renders on GitHub, in Obsidian, or in a chat answer. Diagrams become cheap enough to write for a single paragraph.
  5. Style is global. Mermaid has themes; Pele exposes CSS variables (--pele-series-1…), so every diagram on a site matches the page and follows dark mode without re-rendering. There is no per-diagram art direction, and that is the point.
  6. It is easy for machines to write. The syntax is small enough that language models produce it reliably, which is why Mermaid has become the default way for chat assistants to draw.

The difference in effort is easy to see. Here is the same four-step process in Mermaid and in D3. The D3 version is shortened, and it still has to decide every coordinate:

D3 (abridged)

const steps = ["idea","draft","review","ship"];
const svg = d3.select("body").append("svg")
  .attr("width", 520).attr("height", 60);
const g = svg.selectAll("g").data(steps).join("g")
  .attr("transform", (d, i) => `translate(${i*130},10)`);
g.append("rect").attr("width", 90).attr("height", 40);
g.append("text").attr("x", 45).attr("y", 25)
  .attr("text-anchor", "middle").text(d => d);
svg.selectAll("line").data(steps.slice(1)).join("line")
  .attr("x1", (d, i) => i*130 + 90).attr("y1", 30)
  .attr("x2", (d, i) => i*130 + 130).attr("y2", 30)
  .attr("marker-end", "url(#arrow)");
// …plus the arrow marker, styles, and a resize handler

The D3 version can become a Tufte graphic. The Mermaid version never will, and it took ten seconds.

5The figure: Otto Neurath

Is there a Tufte for standard diagrams? There is no single obvious name, but the strongest candidate, and the style of this page, is Otto Neurath (1882–1945). He was a Viennese economist and philosopher who decided that statistics belonged to everyone, and built a standard picture language so that a worker could read a chart about housing or mortality in a museum without training.

To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures.
A catch-phrase of Neurath’s Vienna museum
1882
Born in Vienna. He studies mathematics, economics and history, and becomes part of the Vienna Circle of logical empiricists. Philosophers still cite “Neurath’s boat”: we rebuild our knowledge plank by plank while staying afloat in it.
1925
Founds the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Wien (Museum of Society and Economy). Its exhibitions explain the city’s statistics with pictures: the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics.
c. 1929
Gerd Arntz, a German woodcut artist, joins and designs the pictograms: flat, frontal, simplified, cut to work in rows. Over the years he draws thousands.
1934
The museum is closed after the Austrian civil war. The team flees to The Hague and sets up the International Foundation for Visual Education. Around 1935 the method is renamed Isotype: International System Of TYpographic Picture Education.
1940
Neurath and Marie Reidemeister escape to England as the Germans invade the Netherlands. They marry in 1941 and found the Isotype Institute in 1942.
1945
Neurath dies. Marie Neurath runs Isotype for another three decades, making picture books for children and information material for newly independent countries.

The Isotype rules

Count by repetitionTen thousand people is ten symbols, never one symbol ten times bigger. Size cannot be read reliably; counting can.
One symbol, one meaningA worker or a car looks the same on every chart, so a reader learns it once.
Glance, then lookThe main point must come at the first glance and the details at the second. If a third look still finds something new, the chart has too much in it.
A transformer in betweenSomeone translates the data into a picture by applying the rules, and does not invent a new design each time.

Set that last rule beside Mermaid. The person who knows the content writes text. The transformer is now a parser and a layout algorithm. The symbols are a fixed library of shapes, and the rules are the notation. Neurath’s workshop has become a function call: render(text).

The credit is shared. Marie Reidemeister was the principal transformer from the start, and Gerd Arntz drew the symbols. Isotype was a team method, which is one more reason it fits this tradition: standard diagrams are about the system, not the author.

Other candidates for the title

Harry BeckThe Underground diagram (1933): a schematic that drops geography so the structure shows. Every transit map since copies it.
Jacques BertinSémiologie graphique (1967). He sits between the two lines, with a grammar of visual variables that serves both.
Daniel Moody“The Physics of Notations” (2009) is the theory of good notations: semiotic clarity, perceptual discriminability, semantic transparency, complexity management, cognitive integration, visual expressiveness, dual coding, graphic economy and cognitive fit. It has often been used to critique UML.
Barbara TverskyA cognitive psychologist who studies why lines, boxes and arrows work so well for ordinary people (Mind in Motion, 2019).
Helen PurchaseHer experiments (1997) measured which layout rules actually help readers. Fewer edge crossings mattered most, which is what layout algorithms optimise.
Simon BrownCreated the C4 model of software architecture: a few levels of zoom (system, containers, components, code), deliberately independent of notation and tooling.

6Where a Nim library fits

A Nim wrapper around Pele sits in the dark corner of the map. Its job is to make the standard diagram free: one line in a nimib page, rendered at build time to SVG that inherits the page’s theme. Tufte-style graphics in Nim are a different library, closer to D3 or Vega-Lite, and both can exist. Neurath would have liked the first one: a transformer anyone can call.
