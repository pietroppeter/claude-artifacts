Diagrams, part three

Ready-made, then made yours

Start from the standard diagram everybody knows, written in plain Mermaid and drawn by Pele. Then theme it, move it, bend its arrows and make it your own, in a separate layer that never touches the standard. Below is a working proof of concept.

1Two layers, one diagram

Part one was Tufte: every graphic made by hand. Part two was Neurath: the standard diagram that nobody has to think about. This page tries to have both. The standard part stays standard: a Mermaid source that renders anywhere, on GitHub or in Obsidian, with Pele’s layout. On top of it sits a personal layer, a small file of choices about that one diagram. It names the theme, nudges a box, bends an arrow, colours a node. Every entry is keyed by the ids that are already in the Mermaid source, so the layer survives when you edit the source.

1 ×SourceMermaid text. Standard, portable, diffable. Says what and which links.
1 ×DrawingPele’s SVG. Every node carries data-id and its position, and every edge carries its id, which is what makes a layer possible.
0–1 ×LayerJSON. Optional and personal. Offsets, not coordinates, so a nudge still means something after the algorithm moves things.
n ×ThemesFonts and colours as Pele CSS variables, plus a few line weights. The same source and layer can be drawn in any of them.

2Try it

Follow the steps, or skip them and play: drag a box, click an arrow to change how it is routed, pick “bézier” and pull its handles, click a node to colour it, or edit either text panel. The diagram is rendered by the real Pele library, so any Mermaid flowchart works.

The same source and the same layer, drawn in every theme. The moves and the bent arrows carry over, because they are stored relative to Pele’s layout, and Pele lays the diagram out again for each font. These copies follow whatever you do above.

4How it would work in Nim and nimib

The process you just followed is a loop, and nimib is a good home for it because a nimib page is already “write code, build, look”. A sketch, with a placeholder name:

import nimib, diagrammi        # placeholder name

nbInit
nbDiagram(layer = "release.layer.json", theme = "isotype"): """
flowchart LR
  idea[Idea] --> draft(Draft)
  draft --> review{Review?}
  review -->|yes| ship((Ship))
"""
nbSave
  1. Write the standard diagram in the notebook. Without a layer it renders like any Mermaid block, and the source still renders on GitHub.
  2. Build. Pele renders the SVG at build time (in a Node process, as the other diagrams thread already does), and the layer is applied to it as a pure transform of the SVG. The published page needs no JavaScript.
  3. Edit. A dev flag (say -d:diagramEdit) adds this page’s editor to every diagram. Dragging and styling write the layer file next to the notebook.
  4. Commit the source and the layer. The diff says “review moved 80 px down” in a file you can read, instead of a binary drawing.

The layer logic is small and deterministic: offsets, the ports a route uses, cubic curves, arrowheads. It could be written once in Nim and compiled twice, natively to apply the layer at build time and with nim js to drive the editor in the browser. A nimib page compiled to JS already showed that pattern working, in the declarative dragging page.

The layer format, as built here

{
  "theme":  "isotype",                 // any theme name
  "arrows": "curve",                   // routing after a move: pele | straight | elbow | curve
  "nodes":  { "ship":   { "dx": -40, "dy": 96, "fill": "#2d5d8c" } },
  "edges":  { "L_review_draft_0": { "route": "bezier", "c1": [10, 90], "c2": [-10, 90] } }
}

Node keys are Mermaid ids. Edge keys are Pele’s edge ids (L_from_to_n), which stay stable as long as an edge keeps its endpoints. Bézier handles are stored relative to the arrow’s two ends, so they follow the nodes. When an id disappears from the source, its entries are listed as orphans instead of being deleted. Offsets have a limit: if an edit makes Pele rearrange the diagram a lot, a nudged box can land on another one. Absolute pins would be the other choice, and a real library would probably offer both. Only flowcharts are draggable in this proof of concept; themes apply to everything Pele draws.

5The figure: Enzo Mari

For a page about taking the industrial and making it personal, the patron is Enzo Mari (1932–2020), the Italian designer who spent his career inside industry and argued with it. In 1974 he published Proposta per un’autoprogettazione (“a proposal for self-design”): drawings of 19 pieces of furniture, chairs, tables, beds and shelves, to be built by anyone from standard pine boards and nails. He offered the plans free to whoever wrote to ask for them. Owners were invited to change the designs and send back photos of what they had made.

1932
Born near Novara. Largely self-taught, he studies at the Brera academy in Milan.
1950s–60s
Programmed art and research on perception, then decades of objects for Danese, Driade, Olivetti and others, along with children’s books and the 16-animal wooden puzzle 16 animali (1957).
1974
Autoprogettazione: an exhibition at Galleria Milano and the book of plans. The point was not cheap furniture. By building a chair you understand why it is made the way it is, and you become able to judge, and change, the design of things.
2010
Artek puts the Sedia 1 chair from the book into production, sold as a kit to assemble.
2020
Dies in Milan in October, days after the opening of a major retrospective at the Triennale.

Autoprogettazione maps closely onto this page. The plan is the standard: anyone can follow it and get a chair that works. Building it yourself is the personal layer: you cut, adjust, paint and keep a record of what you changed. Mari never asked people to design chairs from scratch, only to stop being passive about the ones they used. This page has the look of his book: plain sans type, line drawings, numbered parts lists and the colour of pine.

Other figures for this part

Bret Victor“Inventing on Principle” (2012), Learnable Programming, Dynamicland. He argues that a creator needs an immediate connection to what they create. This editor follows him: drag the picture, watch the data change. His essays also switch visual style to suit each idea, as you noticed.
Ivan SutherlandSketchpad (1963), the first interactive drawing program. Its drawings were made of constraints that the computer kept satisfied while you dragged with a light pen. That is the ancestor of “arrows that stay algorithmic”.
Donald KnuthMetafont (1977–79): one standard design of a typeface, with dozens of parameters that give you your own font. That is the layer idea applied to letters. Literate programming, nimib’s grandparent, is his too.
Alan KayThe Dynabook and Smalltalk: computing as a personal medium you can reach into and change, not a product you consume.
Bruno MunariMari’s elder in Milan, designer of useless machines and children’s books. He thought play is how people learn to design.
Steph AngoPele’s home at Obsidian, and the essay “File over app” (2023): your data should live in files you control, readable without the app. The personal layer is a file over app.
